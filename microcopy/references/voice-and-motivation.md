# Voice, tone, and motivation

Use this reference when the task involves establishing a voice, changing tone, persuading someone to act, or writing copy for a new product without an established content system.

## Build a working voice

Voice is the product's consistent character. Tone is how that character responds to the current situation.

Derive voice from evidence in this order:

1. Existing content standards, brand foundations, and named product values.
2. Repeated language in the product, especially navigation, core objects, and primary actions.
3. Research, interviews, support conversations, reviews, and usability sessions that capture users' own words.
4. A restrained neutral voice when none of the above exists.

For substantial voice work, define a compact working brief:

- **Purpose:** The change the product helps people make.
- **Values:** Three to five traits that must be audible in the language, each translated into concrete writing behavior.
- **Character:** A few useful scales such as warm to reserved, playful to serious, and conversational to formal.
- **Relationship:** The role the product should play, such as capable assistant, peer, specialist, or guide.
- **Vocabulary:** Preferred terms, forbidden ambiguities, and phrases users already use naturally.

Turn abstract traits into decisions. "Practical" might mean lead with the next action, use concrete examples, and keep explanations beside the relevant control. A list of adjectives without behavioral consequences is not a voice guide.

## Understand the person on the other side

The useful audience model is not a demographic sketch. Capture what changes the words:

- the practical problem they need to solve;
- the emotional pressure surrounding it;
- the outcome they hope to reach;
- the objections or concerns that may stop them;
- the benefits they care about, rather than features the company is proud of;
- the expertise and product familiarity they can reasonably be expected to have.

Use their language when reliable research exists. Paraphrase it into the product voice without sanding away the terms that make it recognizable. Never present an assumption as user research.

## Keep the voice, change the tone

| Situation | Useful tone |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery, onboarding, first use | Welcoming, orienting, lightly motivating |
| Routine task | Direct, compact, predictable |
| Waiting | Informative, honest, reassuring |
| Success | Clear, positive, forward-looking |
| User-correctable error | Calm, specific, constructive |
| Product failure | Accountable, plain, recovery-focused |
| Payment, privacy, security, or data loss | Serious, explicit, free of wit |
| Repeated expert workflow | Professional, terse, stable |

Personality should never delay comprehension. A product can be warm without being cute, and professional without sounding legalistic.

## Write conversationally

- Write what a capable person would say aloud in the same situation.
- Address the reader directly when it makes the sentence clearer.
- Prefer active constructions and concrete subjects.
- Replace internal process language with the person's goal and outcome.
- Use questions only when answering them naturally moves the interaction forward.
- Read the string aloud. If it sounds like a form letter, rewrite it.

Conversational writing is edited speech, not casual noise. Keep grammar, meaning, and terminology precise. Use slang or cultural references only for an audience that will understand them and a voice that can carry them.

## Motivate without manipulation

Value is strongest when it answers "What changes for me?" Translate a feature or required action into a relevant outcome, then invite the person to act.

At an important decision point, use one or two of these when they are true and material:

- the benefit of acting;
- the concern that can be resolved;
- the effort, time, price, or commitment involved;
- the fact that a choice can be changed later;
- truthful, current social proof that helps reduce uncertainty.

Keep ordinary operational controls conventional. A button such as "Save" or "Continue" is often clearer than a sales line. Value-focused labels are most useful for entry points and conversion decisions where the benefit is otherwise easy to lose.

Protect the long-term relationship. A short-term lift does not justify confirmshaming, fear, false urgency, or a refusal label that insults the person. The copy should leave them feeling informed even when they choose no.
