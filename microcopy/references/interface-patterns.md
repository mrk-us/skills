# Interface copy patterns

Read only the sections that match the current surface. Each pattern starts with the uncertainty that the copy needs to remove.

## Sign-up, login, and account recovery

For sign-up, answer why an account is useful, how much effort it takes, and what happens to personal information. Keep navigation labels conventional so people can find "Sign up" and "Log in" without decoding brand language. A page heading can carry more voice when its purpose remains obvious.

For social or third-party sign-in, say what access is requested and whether the product can post, message, or share data. Base the reassurance on actual permissions and policy.

Treat returning people like returning people. Keep login familiar and welcoming without slowing it down. Account recovery is a high-intent task, so identify the requested input, say where the recovery message will go, and explain what to do if it does not arrive.

## Subscriptions, permissions, and opt-ins

Answer four questions near the decision:

- What will I receive or enable?
- Why is it useful to me?
- How often will it happen?
- How can I change my mind?

Ask after the person has enough context to judge the offer. State privacy and delivery claims precisely. Avoid preselected consent, vague "updates," and refusal text designed to embarrass.

## Contact and support

Start from why the person arrived. Sales contact copy should make the next conversation concrete. Support copy should acknowledge the problem, route the request, name the information needed, and set an honest response expectation.

After submission, confirm receipt and say what happens next. Never promise a reply time the service cannot meet.

## Errors

An error message should usually contain:

1. What did not happen.
2. Why, when the reason helps the person recover.
3. The exact next action.
4. An alternative or support path when they cannot fix it themselves.

Place field errors beside the field. Preserve entered data. Use the person's task language instead of validation codes, system objects, or developer terminology. Do not blame them or label their input "illegal."

Security can require an intentionally nonspecific explanation. Keep the recovery route specific even when the cause cannot be disclosed. Show diagnostic codes only when they help support staff resolve the incident, and keep them secondary to the human message.

## Confirmations and success

Scale the response to the importance of the action. A small saved state may need only a brief confirmation. A purchase, submission, deletion, account change, or long-running job may need:

- clear certainty about what happened;
- the important result or retained value;
- the next required or useful action;
- timing, destination, or delivery details;
- a reversal path when one exists.

Focus on the person's new state, not a generic "successfully completed" formula. Do not celebrate actions that may feel serious, stressful, or irreversible.

## Empty and zero-result states

Distinguish the cause before writing:

- **First use:** Explain what will live here, why it is useful, and how to add the first item.
- **Cleared state:** Confirm that the absence is expected and offer the likely next action.
- **No search results:** Name the query or filters, then offer a spelling fix, broader search, related result, or filter reset.
- **Unavailable data:** Explain when data will appear or what condition must change.
- **Permission or connection gap:** Name what is missing and provide the route to resolve it.

Do not make a dead end friendlier while leaving it a dead end. Give the person a useful exit.

## Labels, hints, and placeholders

A label identifies what belongs in a field or what a control changes. Keep it visible. A hint explains a non-obvious rule, reason, example, or consequence. Keep essential hints available while the person enters or reviews a value.

A placeholder may show a short example, a useful category, or a lightweight prompt. It should not repeat the label or carry a requirement that disappears on input. Leave the field empty when a placeholder adds no decision value.

For sensitive or unusual questions, explain why the information is needed, whether it is optional, how it will be used, and what the person gains by providing it. Reflect the real data flow and privacy policy.

## Buttons and nearby decision copy

Routine buttons should name familiar actions such as "Save," "Edit," "Upload," or "Continue." Important entry or conversion buttons can pair the action with its relevant result when that improves the decision.

For destructive or consequential actions, use the exact consequence on the primary button, such as "Delete workspace" or "Cancel subscription." Make the secondary choice equally clear. Avoid ambiguous pairs such as "Confirm" and "Cancel" when either word could refer to more than one thing.

Nearby copy can resolve one last concern such as price, trial terms, privacy, reversibility, or saved progress. Use it only when the concern is real and the claim is verified.

## Settings, choices, and toggles

Label a toggle by the state that is true when it is on. Explain effects that are not obvious, including who can see the result, what data is used, what will happen automatically, and whether the change applies retroactively.

For choices with tradeoffs, describe consequences in parallel language. Make defaults and recommended options honest. If people can change the choice later, say where or when.

## Loading, processing, and waiting

Tell people what is happening and whether they can leave, continue elsewhere, retry, or expect a notification. Show real progress when the product knows it. For longer waits, set an honest time expectation or explain the next milestone.

Rotating status messages can make a wait easier to follow, but they must not imply work the system is not doing. If the wait failed or stalled, replace reassurance with recovery.

## Notifications and transient messages

Name the event, its effect, and any required action. A toast is suitable for brief, noncritical feedback. Persistent or consequential information needs a durable location. Do not put recovery instructions in a message that disappears before they can be followed.

## Missing pages and broken routes

Explain in ordinary language that the page cannot be found or is no longer available. Add light empathy, then offer the most useful destinations, search, or support route. Brand character can make the moment memorable, but recovery remains the job.
